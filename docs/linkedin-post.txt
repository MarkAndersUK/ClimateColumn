VERSION A — the physics (~1,350 characters)
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

I built a 1-D radiative-convective atmospheric column model in C#, and
the part I didn't expect was what it took to make CO2 behave properly.

Everyone knows CO2 forcing is logarithmic — each doubling adds roughly
the same warming. What's less obvious is that this is not a
thermodynamic law. It's a property of the shape of the absorption
spectrum, and if you model the atmosphere as a single grey absorber you
cannot reproduce it at all.

Here's the mechanism. At present-day concentrations the core of CO2's
15 µm band is already opaque — adding more changes nothing there. The
forcing comes from the band's flanks, where absorption is still weak.
So the law you get depends entirely on how absorption falls off away
from the band centre. Exponential falloff gives you a logarithm. A
single isolated pressure-broadened line, with its 1/Δν² Lorentz wing,
would give you a square root instead.

That's testable, so I tested it. With a tuned grey absorber, the
model's forcing ratio against the accepted law grows from 1.17 to 2.14
across 285–1000 ppm: the wrong shape, not merely the wrong magnitude.
Replace it with sixteen spectral bands derived from real HITRAN line
data and it straightens out — fitting F = A·ln(C/C₀) gives A between
6.84 and 7.10 W/m² per ln, a 3.6% drift across a 3.5× concentration
range.

The absorber amount is exactly linear in concentration. The logarithm
is produced entirely by the band structure. That's the spectroscopy
doing the work, and it's genuinely satisfying to watch it emerge from
measured line strengths rather than being fitted in.

13,507 lines of C#, .NET 8, more test than source.

VERSION B — short (~650 characters)
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

CO2 forcing is logarithmic — each doubling adds about the same warming.
I'd always filed that under "known fact". Building a radiative-
convective column model in C# taught me it isn't a law at all.

It's a property of the absorption spectrum. The band centre is already
saturated, so the forcing comes from the wings — and the falloff shape
there decides the law. Exponential wings give a logarithm. A single
Lorentz line would give a square root.

Model it as one grey absorber and you can't reproduce it: the forcing
ratio drifts from 1.17 to 2.14 across 285–1000 ppm. Use sixteen bands
derived from HITRAN line data and the response is logarithmic to 3.6% —
with the absorber amount exactly linear in concentration.

The spectroscopy does all the work.

VERSION C — the one I'd actually post: two errors cancelling
(~1,500 characters)
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

My atmospheric model was getting the right answer for the wrong reason,
and it took three failed hypotheses to find out.

Background: CO2 forcing should go as ln(concentration). Mine did, to
2.7% — suspiciously good. I went looking for the residual.

Hypothesis 1: the k-distribution quadrature was too coarse. I wrote the
test that would confirm it — refine the quadrature, watch the error
fall. It doubled.

Hypothesis 2: the band absorption distributions were being extrapolated
away from where they were derived, which the project's own notes warn
against. Fixed that. It got worse again.

Hypothesis 3, and the right one: the line wings were truncated at
15 cm⁻¹ from band centre. That matters because the logarithm *comes
from* the far wings — the band core is saturated, so all the action is
in the flanks. I'd been throwing away the exact part of the spectrum
that produces the effect I was measuring.

But the punchline is what widening the cutoff revealed. Alone, it
pushed the forcing coefficient from 6.99 to 9.35 — badly wrong. The
truncated wings had been silently compensating for a too-coarse band
split. Two substantial errors, cancelling, producing a suspiciously
perfect result.

Properly converged — 16 bands, 16 quadrature points, 400 cm⁻¹ wings,
and a recalibrated absorber amount — the coefficient lands at 6.84–7.10
against a published 5.35. The headline number barely moved: 1.32× to
1.33×. Everything I'd concluded from it was still true.

But it's now true because the numbers are converged, not because two
mistakes happened to agree.

If your numerical result looks unreasonably good, check whether it
survives being refined. Mine didn't.
